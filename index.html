01 · Research

A geospatial–health pipeline

One throughline connects the projects below: measure the environment from space, link it to ground observations, and model how the change drives risk. The study areas move from Gulf Coast cities to entire countries, but the pipeline stays the same.

Mosquito-borne disease and climate

On an NIH NIAID R01, I lead the geospatial and remote-sensing work on Aedes and Culex populations in Houston and Miami. I predict where these mosquitoes thrive by combining Landsat and Sentinel spectral indices, gridded climate (PRISM, GRIDMET), land cover and trap counts in Random Forest, XGBoost and GAM models.

In Mexico, a 40-year analysis showed that dengue outbreaks grow because the virus reaches new places, not because transmission intensifies where it already circulates. In South Carolina I am building an emerging hot-spot and GWR analysis of nearly 30 years of state Culex surveillance.

Climate extremes and child health

With Burroughs Wellcome Fund and NIH NICHD support, I built a county-level big-data pipeline that joins daily temperature, the Evaporative Demand Drought Index (EDDI) and poverty measures to early childhood outcomes across the contiguous U.S.

The maps show where drought and poverty together shape child well-being, and where the relationship is strongest.

Theme C

U.S. counties
incl. Alaska

2010–2023

Space–time patterns of sexually transmitted infections

I built county-level space–time cubes of chlamydia, gonorrhea, early syphilis and primary & secondary syphilis rates for 2010–2023 and ran Emerging Hot Spot Analysis: Getis-Ord Gi* with FDR correction, followed by Mann-Kendall trend tests on each county's z-score series.

The maps separate entrenched burden from new spread. Chlamydia and gonorrhea form persistent and intensifying hot spots across the Gulf South and Carolinas. Early syphilis shows new and consecutive hot spots spreading into Texas and Oklahoma, the Dakotas, and a band through eastern Tennessee, western North Carolina and Kentucky. I paired the clusters with exploratory regressions on County Health Rankings measures of socioeconomics and clinical care.

Spatial criminology and the urban environment

With collaborators in USC's Department of Criminology & Criminal Justice, I apply the same spatial toolkit to crime and policing.

In a six-city study (Baltimore, Boston, Cincinnati, Washington DC, Atlanta and Seattle), I am first author on an analysis of whether tract-level urban heat island exposure predicts violent crime. It uses negative binomial models with population offsets, two independent heat measures (Climate Central and NOAA CAPA Heat Watch), and a random forest with SHAP.

In Denver, I tested whether hate crimes concentrate along neighborhood edges, using 100 m and 200 m census-tract boundary buffers and kernel density surfaces. I also contributed the spatial analysis to a study of field booking arrests and neighborhood disparities in policing.

Theme E

33.3°N 79.2°W
North Inlet–Winyah Bay, SC

1990–2019

Deep learning for coastal wetlands

My doctoral work mapped South Carolina's 340,000 acres of salt marsh with a U-Net trained on Sentinel-2 imagery, separating low marsh (Spartina alterniflora) from high marsh (Juncus roemerianus) statewide.

An autoencoder then reconstructed 20 years of Landsat NDVI trajectories to flag dieback pixels, (see the year-by-year animation), and Getis-Ord Gi* hot-spot analysis tied 30 years of dieback to drought anomalies, salinity and turbidity.

Theme F

Current direction

Earth foundation models for surveillance

I am adapting pretrained geospatial foundation models with LoRA fine-tuning and multi-task learning to detect and forecast invasive disease vectors from satellite and weather data. This work is the basis of a NASA Accelerating Earth Solutions proposal on which I am Co-PI.

  • Geospatial foundation models
  • LoRA
  • Multi-task learning

Lab-to-field tools

I also co-developed AeEggCounter, a YOLO-based smartphone app that counts Aedes eggs on ovitrap linings for high-throughput surveillance. Trained on 4,398 annotated eggs, it reached 88.3% precision, 91.7% recall and an F1 score of 89.9% (J. Am. Mosquito Control Assoc. 42(3), 2026).
